## What Is Small Business Bookkeeping?

Small business bookkeeping is the systematic process of recording, categorizing, and reconciling every financial transaction that occurs within a business. In Saskatoon and across Saskatchewan, routine bookkeeping transforms disorganized bank statements, receipts, and invoices into structured financial ledgers that reflect the true financial health of your enterprise.

Typical day-to-day bookkeeping responsibilities include:

- Recording daily customer sales, point-of-sale deposits, and electronic receivables.
- Tracking operating expenditures, vendor disbursements, and recurring subscriptions.
- Organizing and digitizing supplier receipts and commercial invoices.
- Monitoring accounts receivable and issuing friendly invoice payment reminders.
- Reconciling business chequing accounts, credit card accounts, and merchant gateways monthly.
- Calculating provincial sales tax (PST at 6%) and federal goods and services tax (GST at 5%) collections and input tax credits (ITCs).
- Maintaining organized documentation for annual [T2 corporate tax filing administration](/services#tax).

While bookkeeping and accounting are closely connected, they serve distinct functions. Bookkeeping focuses on the ongoing, administrative recording and reconciliation of daily business transactions. Accounting interprets those compiled financial records to prepare year-end adjustments, generate financial compilation reports, and advise on corporate tax strategies. Clean bookkeeping provides the essential groundwork that accountants and tax administrators require.

## What Records Must a Saskatoon Business Keep for CRA Compliance?

The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) mandates that every Saskatchewan business maintain complete source documents and supporting records for all business income, allowable deductions, and tax credits for a minimum of six years from the end of the relevant tax year. If you file a return late, records must be kept for six years from the actual date the return was submitted to the CRA.

Saskatchewan entrepreneurs often ask: *can I keep business receipts digitally in Saskatchewan?* Under CRA guidelines, electronic and digitized receipts are fully accepted as legal source documentation, provided they are legible, accessible, unalterable, and backed up in secure cloud storage.

| Record Category | Mandatory Source Documents | CRA Retention Window | Audit Risk Level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gross Business Revenue | Invoices issued, cash register slips, POS merchant logs, bank deposit summaries | 6 full tax years | High if deposits exceed declared revenue |
| Operating Deductions | Vendor receipts, commercial bills, cancelled payments, digital expense receipts | 6 full tax years | Very High for travel, meals, and vehicle use |
| Banking & Financing | Monthly bank statements, corporate credit card statements, loan agreements | 6 full tax years | High during complete financial reconciliations |
| Fixed Assets & Equipment | Vehicle purchase agreements, machinery bills, commercial lease contracts | Lifetime of asset + 6 years | Moderate to High for depreciation schedules |
| Payroll & Subcontractors | T4 slips, payroll summaries, source deduction remittances, subcontractor contracts | 6 full tax years | Critical under CRA payroll audit programs |
| Corporate Legal Records | Certificate of incorporation, corporate bylaws, share registry, director registers | Permanent corporate life | Mandatory under provincial ISC compliance |

Review official federal recordkeeping expectations directly via the [CRA business records guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/keeping-records.html).

## Sole Proprietor Bookkeeping vs. Bookkeeping for Newly Incorporated Businesses

The primary difference between sole proprietor bookkeeping and bookkeeping for newly incorporated businesses is that corporations are separate legal entities requiring distinct corporate bank accounts, balance sheet reconciliations, and formal shareholder loan tracking. Sole proprietorships report business income directly on the owner's personal tax return using CRA Form T2125, whereas corporations must produce structured trial balances for federal corporate T2 tax filings.

Many growing Saskatchewan entrepreneurs start as sole proprietors and transition into an incorporated entity to take advantage of the combined federal-provincial small business corporate tax rate. Knowing how your bookkeeping requirements change during this transition is vital for staying compliant.

| Bookkeeping Dimension | Sole Proprietorship in Saskatchewan | Incorporated Saskatchewan Corporation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal Separation | None; the owner and business are legally identical | Distinct legal entity under Saskatchewan legislation |
| Banking Requirements | Separate account recommended; commingling common | Mandatory separate corporate bank and credit accounts |
| Financial Statements | Basic statement of revenue and expenses (Form T2125) | Comprehensive income statement and balance sheet |
| Owner Compensation | Direct owner draws (no payroll tax deductions) | Salary via payroll (T4 slip) or dividends (T5 slip) |
| Shareholder Loans | Not applicable | Mandatory tracking to avoid CRA deemed income rules |
| Annual Regulatory Filing | Personal T1 tax return by June 15 (taxes due April 30) | Corporate T2 return + [ISC annual returns](/blog/saskatchewan-business-registration-isc-annual-returns) |
| Complexity Level | Moderate; focuses on deductible receipt management | High; requires formal ledger reconciliation and GIFI mapping |

## What Are the Most Common Small Business Bookkeeping Mistakes in Saskatoon?

The most common small business bookkeeping mistake in Saskatoon is commingling personal and business transactions in the same personal bank account, which complicates tax deduction verifications and undermines corporate limited liability protections. Commingled accounts create substantial confusion during CRA reviews and drive up accounting costs significantly.

Other frequent bookkeeping errors observed across Saskatchewan small businesses include:

- **Discarding Paper Receipts Without Backups:** Thermal paper receipts fade within months. Failing to scan or photograph receipts leaves business owners without proof during a CRA audit.
- **Neglecting Monthly Bank Reconciliations:** Skipping regular reconciliations allows unrecognized bank charges, duplicate vendor entries, and missed customer receivables to go unnoticed.
- **Falling Behind on Bookkeeping Catch-Up:** Allowing four to six months of paperwork to accumulate turns an easy monthly routine into an overwhelming bottleneck that delays filing deadlines.
- **Misclassifying Capital Assets as Immediate Expenses:** Purchasing heavy equipment or office computers must be depreciated under CRA Capital Cost Allowance (CCA) rules rather than expensed in a single month.
- **Ignoring Outstanding Invoices (Accounts Receivable):** Inconsistent accounts receivable monitoring leads to overdue client accounts, poor cash liquidity, and unrecoverable bad debts.
- **Confusing Tax Filings with Registry Compliance:** Filing sales tax returns or corporate taxes does not satisfy your provincial corporate registry filing obligations with Information Services Corporation (ISC).

## Step-by-Step Monthly Bookkeeping Checklist for Saskatchewan Owners

A dependable monthly bookkeeping routine saves countless hours and keeps your business audit-proof. Follow this eight-step checklist at the close of every month:

1. **Capture & Digitize All Paper Receipts:** Snap high-resolution mobile photos or scan paper receipts immediately, tagging vendor name, date, total paid, and applicable sales tax.
2. **Import & Categorize Bank and Card Activity:** Review all incoming deposits and outgoing withdrawals against your standard chart of accounts.
3. **Reconcile Commercial Bank Accounts:** Match every cleared transaction on your monthly bank and credit card statements against your accounting ledgers to confirm a zero-dollar variance.
4. **Calculate GST and PST Remittances:** Calculate total GST (5%) collected against Input Tax Credits (ITCs) paid, and track taxable Saskatchewan sales subject to Provincial Sales Tax (6%).
5. **Review Accounts Receivable Aging:** Identify past-due client accounts exceeding 30, 60, or 90 days and send polite follow-up payment statements.
6. **Reconcile Payroll & Source Deductions:** Ensure employee wage withholdings (CPP, EI, and income tax) match monthly CRA source deduction remittances.
7. **Document Shareholder Contributions and Draws:** Keep an exact log of any personal funds contributed to the corporation or funds withdrawn as dividends or salary.
8. **Generate Monthly Financial Statements:** Review your Profit & Loss statement and Balance Sheet to evaluate monthly net margin and maintain cloud backups of all records.

### Can Saskatchewan small businesses keep digital receipts instead of paper?
Yes, under Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) guidelines, Saskatchewan businesses are legally permitted to store financial records and receipts electronically. Digital receipts, scanned paper invoices, and electronic bank records are fully valid for CRA tax deduction audits as long as the digital copies remain clear, easily readable, unaltered, and securely backed up for the mandatory six-year retention period.

### When does a Saskatchewan business need to register for GST and PST?
A Saskatchewan business must register for a federal GST account with the CRA once its gross worldwide taxable revenue exceeds \$30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters or within a single calendar quarter. However, Saskatchewan Provincial Sales Tax (PST at 6%) operates under different provincial rules: businesses that sell taxable goods or provide taxable services in Saskatchewan must register with the Saskatchewan Ministry of Finance regardless of sales volume, as Saskatchewan does not have a small supplier revenue exemption for PST.

### What is the difference between bookkeeping and accounting for a small business?
Bookkeeping focuses on the day-to-day administrative recording of financial transactions, including logging expenses, issuing customer invoices, reconciling bank accounts, and maintaining receipt archives. Accounting takes those organized records and analyzes the broader financial trends, prepares statutory year-end financial compilations, calculates tax liabilities, and advises on corporate tax optimization.
